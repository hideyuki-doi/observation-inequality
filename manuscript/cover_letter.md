Dear Editor,

I am pleased to submit the manuscript "Occurrence records follow described species, not individuals, and cannot detect declines in most small animals" for consideration as a Research Article in *PNAS* (Biological Sciences, Ecology).

Occurrence records aggregated by the Global Biodiversity Information Facility now number almost four billion and are increasingly used to judge whether species are declining, including invertebrates, for which structured monitoring is scarce. Yet how these records are distributed relative to the amount of animal life, and which declines they can reveal, has not been quantified. Building on the global censuses of biomass and abundance published in *PNAS* and elsewhere in recent years, the manuscript compares 3.89 billion GBIF records with census abundances of ten animal groups, from soil nematodes to mesopelagic fishes, and then asks how often a species must be recorded for a population decline to become detectable.

The main findings are as follows. Records per individual differ by about 13 orders of magnitude between soil nematodes and mammals, but this difference does not arise because large animals are easier to see: across groups, records are unrelated to abundance and body mass and instead rise with the number of described species, with birds and mammals receiving far more records than invertebrate groups of similar species richness. Because annual record counts are strongly overdispersed even after removing species trends, detecting a 30% decline per decade takes 16 to 51 years for the most frequently recorded species, and record-based trends of British butterflies agree only moderately with a structured monitoring scheme. Applied to every described species, these requirements imply that a decline would remain undetectable within a century for more than 90% of described nematode, mite and copepod species, compared with fewer than 1% of bird species.

I believe these results are of broad interest to readers of *PNAS*. They concern the evidence base for current debates about invertebrate decline and for the monitoring framework of the Kunming–Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework, and they show that an absence of detected decline in occurrence records says little about the small animals that dominate soil and marine ecosystems and sustain decomposition, nutrient cycling and marine food webs. The manuscript also gives a practical rule for when record-based trend claims are informative.

The study was preregistered before the main quantity was computed. The manuscript reports the preregistered results together with an analysis that overturns the interpretation of one of them, and all deviations from the plan are listed in the *SI Appendix*. Code, the preregistration, derived data and internal pre-submission review reports with responses are publicly available at https://github.com/hideyuki-doi/observation-inequality, and the GBIF data are cited by DOI. The use of an AI system (Claude, Anthropic) to assist with code, analyses and drafting is described in the Materials and Methods.
